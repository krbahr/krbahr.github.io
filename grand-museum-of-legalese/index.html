Legal English is one of the most remarkable linguistic achievements in human history.

It has governed property, trials, and trillion-dollar markets across centuries and continents. This catalogue doesn’t argue legalese has no value — it argues the profession deserves a language as modern as the tools now being built for it.

A 2023 MIT study found that lawyers themselves prefer plain English and rate it equally enforceable. The dominant explanation for legalese’s persistence: pure tradition — the “magic spell hypothesis,” where convoluted language signals authority like archaic terms in ritual incantations.

“After six hundred years of lawsuits caused by language atrocities, a terrible suspicion is born. Maybe the lawyers don’t understand each other.”

— David Mellinkoff, The Language of the Law (1963)

This project was sparked by Nir Golan’s observation: “Legalese is the programming language for lawyers.” A brilliant framing — except this programming language hasn’t been refactored since 1066, runs on three legacy compilers, and its documentation was written by monks paid by the word.

Scholars who led the case for modernization:

David Mellinkoff — UCLA. The Language of the Law (1963). The founding text.
Richard Wydick — UC Davis. Plain English for Lawyers (1979). The practical guide.
Bryan Garner — Editor of Black’s Law Dictionary. Co-author with Justice Scalia.
Joseph Kimble — Western Michigan. 50+ empirical studies on plain language.
Ross Guberman — Point Made, Point Taken, and the BriefCatch platform.

“The language of the law should not be different without a reason.”

— David Mellinkoff

How This Was Built

A collaboration between lawyer and LLM

This wasn’t one-shotted. The Grand Museum of Legalese was built through extended collaboration between Kyle Bahr (lawyer and legal tech builder) and Claude Opus 4.6 by Anthropic.

The process began with a 75-minute deep research session in which Claude analyzed over 550 sources — law review articles, legal dictionaries, corpus linguistics studies, bar association publications, and historical records — to compile the initial catalogue of 100 specimens with accurate etymologies, case citations, and historical context.

From there, over a dozen iterative rounds of design, editorial refinement, tone calibration, source verification, and feature development produced the glossary, analytics, timeline, and Res Ipsa Bracketur bracket game. Every entry was reviewed for accuracy and voice. The snark was calibrated to honor the tradition while making the case for evolution. The bracket seeding follows authentic NCAA tournament structure.

The result is what happens when domain expertise meets AI capability — not replacement, but genuine collaboration. Neither the lawyer nor the LLM could have built this alone.
