Prologue

A 960-year-old programming language.

Legaltech builder Nir Golan wrote on LinkedIn that “legalese is the programming language for lawyers.” He meant it as a compliment to the precision. He was also, accidentally, pointing at the joke: every working lawyer is still shipping production code in a 960-year-old dialect that nobody compiles, nobody tests, and nobody has refactored since William the Conqueror docked at Pevensey.

When the Normans took England in 1066, they brought with them a French-speaking aristocracy, a French-speaking court system, and a Latin-speaking clergy to handle the paperwork. The locals kept their Old English. For roughly three centuries — and in patches of the law, nearly seven — the English courtroom ran on three different languages at once. Out of that linguistic accident came the vocabulary our contracts still use today.

In 2023, a team at MIT, publishing in PNAS, asked why this persists. Their answer was not what anyone expected. It wasn’t precision. It wasn’t tradition. It was something closer to ritual — what the researchers called the magic spell hypothesis. When non-lawyers were asked to write laws, they instinctively reached for the same contorted, center-embedded prose as lawyers do. The form signals “this is legal.” The mystery is the point.

This codex is an affectionate inventory of the specimens. Some earn their keep — habeas corpus, res ipsa loquitur, stare decisis — concepts too fundamental to rename. Others exist only because the scribe who wrote them in 1385 charged by the word, and nobody has bothered to edit since.

A few — the fertile octogenarian, hereunto set my hand, witnesseth — are here for your enjoyment. Grab a coffee. The tournament begins in Round One.

About This Codex

The Credits

This codex was born from a LinkedIn post by Nir Golan, a legaltech builder who observed that “legalese is the programming language for lawyers.” Nir’s framing captured something true: lawyers, like developers, inherit a codebase. Unlike developers, they rarely refactor.

It also draws heavily on the 2023 PNAS study by Martínez, Mollica, and Gibson at MIT, which found that legalese persists not because it is precise but because it signals authority — a kind of linguistic magic spell. Their conclusion is what every plain-language reformer has suspected for sixty years: the complexity isn’t load-bearing. It’s ceremonial.

The case for reform

This project is not anti-legalese. It is anti-unnecessary legalese. Habeas corpus, stare decisis, res ipsa loquitur — these are doctrines, not decoration. They earn their Latin. But “witnesseth,” “hereunto set my hand,” and the three-word phrase meaning “stop” — these are habits. They survive because nobody in 1847 bothered to modernize the template, and nobody in 1972 bothered, and nobody in 2003 bothered, and here we are.

The profession is building AI tools that draft, review, and negotiate contracts in minutes. It deserves a language as modern as the tools themselves.

How this was built

Human-AI collaboration. Kyle Bahr (AI Innovation Manager at Cleary Gottlieb) set the scope, voice, and editorial boundaries. Claude, from Anthropic, drafted, researched, cross-checked, and coded. Every specimen was sourced against Cornell’s Legal Information Institute, Bryan Garner’s LawProse, David Mellinkoff’s tributes, the SEC’s Plain English Handbook, and the original MIT/PNAS work. The absurdity and necessity ratings are editorial judgments — honest, but subjective. Disagree loudly; the Marx Brothers would have approved.

This artifact is a single HTML file. No tracking, no ads, no paywall. If it made you laugh or gave you one weapon against the next draft of “provided, however, that,” it has done its job.
