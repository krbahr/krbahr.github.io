The case, in 90 seconds

The 2026 federal civil trial between huge AI personalities hinged on trust.

In December 2015, Elon Musk and Sam Altman launched OpenAI as a Delaware non-profit. The Charter said the organization’s “primary fiduciary duty is to humanity.” Musk pledged a billion dollars; he eventually delivered roughly $38 million.

By 2018 the founders had broken; by 2019 OpenAI had spun up a “capped-profit” subsidiary and taken a billion-dollar check from Microsoft; by 2023 Microsoft was in for tens of billions and the OpenAI nonprofit board had fired Altman and rehired him in a single weekend.

In February 2024 Musk sued, and eventually got to the federal courthouse in Oakland — to Judge Yvonne Gonzalez Rogers, a nine-person jury, three weeks of testimony, and a verdict form with five claims pleaded against three named defendants and twenty corporate affiliates. The trial closed on May 14, 2026.

The jury is, as of this writing, deliberating.

The plaintiffs’ theory is simple and biblical: they stole a charity. The defendants’ theory is the inverse: Musk got into a power struggle, lost, started his own AI company, and is now using the federal court as a competitive weapon.

The experiment

Watch a simulated jury make up its mind in slow motion.

In trial work, you only see the verdict, not the deliberation. The jury arrives at a number, the lawyers walk out into the hallway with theories about what mattered and what didn’t, but no one outside the jury room actually knows. The verdict is a single event; the trial that produced it was three weeks of pivotal inflection points and strategic recalibrations.

The premise of this experiment is to use Claude Cowork — an AI workspace with file access, browser access, and structured reasoning — to make those inflection points visible and understand their impact on the decision-makers: the jurors.

We built nine fictional-but-rigorously-calibrated juror profiles, informed by voir dire reporting and Bay Area demographics (each carrying a parameter vector covering mission integrity, commercial necessity, Musk-motive penalty, Altman-trust penalty, document weight, demeanor weight, and remedy aversion).

We assembled the actual trial corpus — 1,225 docket filings via CourtListener, the 254 trial-admitted exhibits from the parties’ public Box folder via evidence.mts.now, and the 12 daily trial pages (digests for Days 2–12 plus the Day 13 live transcript) from trial.mts.now.

Then we ran the trial — day by day — through each juror’s profile, recording where every belief moved and why.

Below is what came out. This is not a prediction of the actual jury’s verdict. Nor is it a substitute for jury research.

It’s a demonstration that an AI workspace can, with appropriate inputs, illuminate the structure of how a particular evidence record will land on a particular kind of panel — the kind of analysis that, before Gen AI, was the exclusive province of high-end trial consultants.

Four days that did almost all the work.

The deliberation

How a polarized panel found its compromise.

Walking into deliberation, the simulated panel was split: seven jurors leaned toward finding OpenAI Inc. corporately liable for breach of charitable trust, but only six were willing to extend that liability to Altman personally, and two — the firmly defense-anchored software engineer (J5) and the retired aerospace operations manager (J2) — were locked at refusal on both.

Unanimity required movement. The trial had produced anchors strong enough that no juror was going to abandon them without a counterweight.

What broke the stalemate was the foreperson — J1, the Santa Clara product operations manager — putting an explicit trade on the table. The plaintiff anchors (J3, J4, J6, J9) would vote NO on Altman individual liability if the defense anchors (J2, J5) would vote YES on the corporation.

Both sides got what they cared most about. The plaintiff side got the institutional finding — the corporation breached its trust, the public is the beneficiary, the judge can fashion equitable relief. The defense side got the personal-liability NOs that protected the named individuals from money judgments, and the clean defense win on Unjust Enrichment.

That is what charitable-trust doctrine permits and what fraud doctrine does not. In a charitable-trust theory, the corporation is the trustee; the credibility hits taken by individual officers can be absorbed into corporate liability without requiring proof that any specific officer committed the specific misrepresentation.

The biggest insight is that Plaintiff’s decision to lead with breach of charitable trust — rather than with fraud or with the more controversial claims — was the doctrinal choice that made the trial-craft work. At least it did so in this simulation.

After the verdict · May 18, 2026

What the real jury decided in under two hours.

In less than two hours, the real jury came back unanimously for the defense — entirely on statute-of-limitations grounds. The breach-of-charitable-trust claim was time-barred under a three-year statute; unjust enrichment under a two-year statute; the Microsoft aiding-and-abetting claim fell the same way. Judge Gonzalez Rogers accepted the advisory verdict and dismissed all of Musk’s claims as untimely. Musk has said he will appeal.

Our simulation predicted differently. Pattern B — a corporate breach-of-charitable-trust win — was our most probable outcome at roughly forty percent. The actual verdict looks closest to Pattern C, our full-defense scenario at fifteen percent.

But the sensitivity analysis flagged the continuous-accrual jury instruction as the single most outcome-determinative element of the case, with a roughly eighty-percent verdict-flip rating. The real case turned on exactly that point. The simulation called the wrong winner on the gate — it called the gate itself, and the single hinge on it, correctly.

We were never trying to predict the verdict. We were trying to see whether an AI workspace can help a litigator identify the inflection points before they happen — and decide where to spend her best advocacy. The simulation can be wrong on the verdict and still be right on the strategy.

Honest limits

Five things this simulation cannot do.

  1. 1. It cannot predict the actual jury’s verdict. The jurors are fictional. The evidence record is real, but the testimony is reconstructed from secondary sources (Trial Sheets + MTS digests) rather than from official transcripts. The deliberation simulation is a model of how a panel composed this way would resolve a case structured this way, not a forecast.
  2. 2. It cannot replace empirical jury research. Mock juries, shadow juries, and post-verdict interviews remain the gold standard for understanding lay juror behavior. AI-as-juror is a thinking aid, not an empirical method, and it does not solve the LLM-projection problem.
  3. 3. It cannot model courtroom demeanor in any rigorous way. Witness credibility is a function of tone, hesitation, eye contact, sweat. We captured “Sam was not always candid” as a moment, but we could not see how Murati delivered it. Real jurors are watching live theater.
  4. 4. It cannot account for the randomness of real deliberation. Real juries deadlock for reasons that have nothing to do with the evidence — personality conflicts, fatigue, life circumstances. The simulation produces a tidy compromise because the simulated jurors are tidy actors.
  5. 5. It cannot validate itself. The convergence between the simulation’s Pattern B verdict and the a priori prediction of the original juror profile document is coherence, not evidence. The framework predicts what it was set up to predict.

For trial lawyers

What this kind of analysis is actually good for.

The temptation, looking at an exercise like this, is to ask whether the AI got the verdict right. That is the wrong question, and the wrong test. The actual question is whether the analysis surfaces things a litigator wouldn’t otherwise see — and surfaces them before they become irreversible at trial.

Consider three things this run made visible:

  1. 1. The witness order. Plaintiff’s witness order functioned as a prosecutorial impeachment ladder against Altman: Murati, then Toner, then McCauley, then Sutskever, all before Altman took the stand. By the time Altman testified, the panel already had a four-witness prior on his credibility; his Day 12 “I don’t recall” cross then confirmed the prior rather than establishing it. That’s a textbook trial-craft choice — but it’s the kind of choice a junior associate could miss if they were charting witnesses by topic instead of by impeachment strategy.
  2. 2. The doctrine choice. Plaintiff led with breach of charitable trust, not with fraud. In charitable-trust doctrine, the corporation is the trustee and individual officers’ credibility hits can be absorbed into institutional liability without specific-intent proof. In fraud, you can’t make that transfer — you’d need to prove a specific actor made a specific knowing misrepresentation to a specific recipient who reasonably relied. The compromise verdict the simulation produced is only available under breach of charitable trust, not under fraud. The doctrinal architecture is what made the verdict shape possible.
  3. 3. The continuous-accrual jury instruction. Defense had a clean statute-of-limitations bar on the evidence — Musk’s September 2020 “captured by Microsoft” tweet plainly put him on notice more than four years before the operative complaint. But the continuous-accrual instruction, applied through the October 2025 Watershed PBC conversion as a fresh breach within the limitations window, undid the bar for the rule-disciplined jurors. The case dies at the gate without that instruction. Defense almost certainly should have fought it harder than the record suggests they did.

None of these are verdict predictions. They are structural observations about how this evidence record interacts with this panel composition under this doctrine. That kind of observation, generated overnight by an AI workspace from a set of structured inputs, is the use case worth taking seriously. It’s not the work of a trial consultant. It’s the work that lets a trial consultant focus on the work that requires human judgment.

Under the hood

How the corpus was assembled.

The entire pipeline ran inside one Claude Cowork project. The docket was pulled from CourtListener via its REST API. The 254 trial exhibits came from the parties’ public Box folder per Pretrial Order No. 1 (Dkt. 446 and Dkt. 505-1), then were reconciled with the curated catalog at evidence.mts.now. The 12 daily trial pages from trial.mts.now were captured via headless Chromium because the wiki is a React SPA that doesn’t render to plain HTML.

The simulation itself ran in five phases: cold-read priors per juror per question, day-by-day evidence updates with witness-credibility tracking, closings and jury instructions, multi-round deliberation with foreperson brokering, and a final sensitivity pass. Every phase persisted structured state to disk so any phase could be re-run with different assumptions without rebuilding the prior phases.

The complete set of working files — pipeline scripts, phase reports, state JSONs, and the original juror-profile document — sits in a single project folder, reproducible by any user with the same Cowork project setup.
